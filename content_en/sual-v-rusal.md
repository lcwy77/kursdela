On November 10, the Economic Disputes Chamber of Russia's Supreme Court will hear a dispute that matters well beyond Rusal: must the board of a Russian company check shareholder proposals against the rules of the foreign exchange where its shares trade?

**The parties.** SUAL holds 25.52% of Rusal's voting shares. Rusal has re-registered in Russia, but its shares still trade on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange.

**How it started.** In August 2024, SUAL demanded an extraordinary shareholders' meeting with three items. A dividend for the first half of the year was put on the agenda without objection. The other two concerned amendments to the charter and to the regulations on the company's governing bodies, including how the company deals with key subsidiaries. Rusal's board refused to put them to a vote.

**SUAL's position.** According to a shareholder representative quoted by Kommersant, the amendments were meant to improve corporate governance and allow minority shareholders to nominate representatives to the boards of key subsidiaries. A source told the newspaper the proposal concerned the rights of shareholders with stakes of 10% or more.

**Rusal's position.** The company believes SUAL's proposals were an attempt to gain control rights disproportionate to its stake and would have gone beyond the reasonable competence of the shareholders' meeting. Rusal's charter refers to Hong Kong Stock Exchange listing rules, and Hong Kong lawyers concluded that the proposed wording contradicted those rules. Rusal expects the court to take into account the principle that shareholders' rights should be proportionate to their stakes.

**What the Bank of Russia and the courts decided.** Acting on SUAL's complaint, the Bank of Russia found in January 2025 that the board's refusal was unlawful, identified a violation of Article 15.23.1 of the Code of Administrative Offenses and issued Rusal a warning. The courts then diverged. SUAL's claim against the board decision itself was dismissed: the courts found the refusal lawful by applying the Hong Kong rules. But in the case in which Rusal challenged the Bank of Russia's ruling, the first instance and the cassation court found the refusal improper and the regulator's ruling lawful. It is this appeal by Rusal that the Supreme Court will hear. The Bank of Russia does not comment on litigation.

**What lawyers say.** Opinions are divided. Stanislav Danilov, a partner at Pen & Paper: "The legislator does not specify to what extent exchange rules may apply," and he finds it strange to place them above the law. Vadim Borodkin, a partner at Orchards, believes the board was entitled to review the amendments and that seeking an opinion from Hong Kong lawyers was done in good faith. David Kononov, managing partner of the MUR law firm, believes the courts should have compared the specific amendments with specific exchange requirements, which the rulings do not do.

**Why it matters.** The Supreme Court's decision will set a rule for every company that has re-registered in Russia but kept a foreign listing: what weighs more when a charter refers to foreign rules and Russian law protects a shareholder's rights. For SUAL and Rusal, it is also a question of influence over the group's key assets.

Rusal has another major dispute, with Rio Tinto, over $1.278 billion and a stake in the Oyu Tolgoi copper project: [our analysis](https://kursmedia.com/en/articles/rusal-v-rio-tinto.html).

**Update, October 15, 2026. A new front: the Boguchany smelter.** On October 15, at the Russian Energy Week forum, SUAL Partners and RusHydro signed a cooperation agreement on the Boguchany aluminum smelter (BoAZ). The smelter is part of the Boguchany Energy and Metals Complex, which Rusal and RusHydro own in equal shares. As SUAL told RBC (as reported by Vedomosti), the parties may gain the right to sell part of the smelter's metal on their own, about 150,000 tonnes a year, including to SUAL's Kamensk-Uralsky Metallurgical Works and to buyers abroad. SUAL may also take part in supplying raw materials to the smelter and, together with RusHydro, develop a program to improve its efficiency. RusHydro will separately consider selling or swapping its stake in BoAZ.

Published reports contain no official reaction from Rusal to the agreement. Rusal chief executive Evgeny Nikitin had earlier said the company was discussing deals over its stake in the energy holding with RusHydro, including a possible increase of its stake in the complex. RBC's source believes the agreement with SUAL could become leverage in those talks; that is the source's assessment, not a confirmed fact.

**Update, October 22, 2026. A dispute over the price of electricity.** On October 12, the Boguchany hydropower plant (3 GW) filed a claim in the Moscow Arbitration Court against the Boguchany aluminum smelter to terminate their electricity and capacity supply contract. Kommersant reported the claim on October 22, and the plant confirmed it. A preliminary hearing is scheduled for February 2027.

According to the plant, the smelter pays 0.5 rubles per kWh, five times below the market and below the station's generation cost; the price has not been revised for years, and the smelter, while economically successful, refuses to pay more. According to Kommersant's sources, RusHydro is seeking a price of about 1.2 rubles per kWh. The smelter consumes about a quarter of the plant's output.

Rusal said that, as an equal shareholder in the plant, it had not approved the claim. The contract was signed in 2012 and runs until 2030, when a loan from VEB.RF matures; the company supports the existing arrangements.

Analysts' estimates: according to Gazprombank (Nikolai Salamov), raising the price to 1.2 rubles would give the plant an additional 2.9 billion rubles in annual revenue; Eyler (Evgenia Franke) estimates that moving the smelter to the wholesale market would cost it about 6 billion rubles a year, and a price of 1.2 rubles about 3 billion. The Industrial Code fund (Maxim Shaposhnikov) estimates the rise in BoAZ's costs at 4–5%, and less than 0.5% for Rusal as a whole.

The claim was filed against the backdrop of RusHydro's agreement with SUAL Partners on the Boguchany smelter (see the October 15 update) and reports that RusHydro is considering selling or swapping its stake in the smelter.

**Update, October 30, 2026. A claim in London.** The dispute has moved beyond Russian courts. Rusal minority shareholders, Sual Partners, linked to Viktor Vekselberg, and Aluminvest Holding, have filed a claim in the High Court in London, Kommersant reported, citing case materials. There are 11 defendants. They include En+ and Rusal, En+ board chairman Christopher Burnham, Rusal board chairman Bernard Zonneveld, Rusal chief executive Evgeny Nikitin, former En+ chairman Lord Gregory Barker, as well as an engineering company and a rail operator whose names have not been disclosed. The newspaper also names Oleg Deripaska as being on the opposing side of the dispute.

The substance of the claims has not been disclosed in public materials. Lawyers interviewed by Kommersant suggest, based on the list of defendants, that the case may concern Rusal's transactions and their terms — price, conflicts of interest or losses. These are their assumptions, not stated claims. According to the newspaper, at the end of June Sual Partners owned 22.52% of Rusal and En+ 56.88%, and Deripaska controls 35% of En+'s voting shares. Aluminvest owned 6.7% at the end of June 2025. Published reports do not include the positions of En+, Rusal or the claimants.

The conflict now has two venues: on November 10 the Supreme Court's Economic Disputes Chamber will hear the dispute over the board's refusal to put SUAL's proposals to a vote, and a new case is beginning in London. This page will be updated.

**Update, November 5, 2026. En+ seeks to recover the costs of the London arbitration from SUAL.** The conflict has gained a third venue. On November 2, En+ applied to the Kaliningrad Region Arbitration Court to recognize and enforce an award of the London Court of International Arbitration (LCIA), Kommersant reported on November 5. The application has not yet been accepted for consideration.

This concerns a different, earlier dispute. In August 2024, SUAL Partners demanded $345 million from En+ in damages over dividends that Rusal did not declare, arguing that En+, as the controlling shareholder, had breached the shareholders' agreement. In September 2026, the arbitrators rejected the claim in full, found it manifestly without merit and ordered the claimant to pay costs: £778,820 and $3.18 million plus 5% annual interest until payment, about 340 million rubles by the newspaper's calculation. According to En+, this is about 2.5 times less than its actual costs of the proceedings.

En+ cites the arbitrators' findings: the losses were not proven, calculations based on modeling the value of Rusal's shares were speculative, and Rusal's board acted independently and in the company's interests. A representative of SUAL Partners did not comment. Rusal said it did not take part in the arbitration and is not a party to En+'s application. The company last paid dividends for the first half of 2022, $302 million; in September 2026, shareholders holding 62.7% of shares supported forgoing dividends for the first half of the year, while 32.2% voted against.

Lawyers interviewed by Kommersant assess the chances differently. Sergei Uchitel (Pen & Paper) considers En+'s position strong: a Russian court may not review the dispute on the merits or the amount of costs. Alexander Matveev (ANP Zenit) allows that SUAL Partners may try to challenge recognition, for example by arguing that the arbitration agreement is invalid. If the award is recognized, enforcement could target the minority shareholder's accounts or securities, including Rusal shares; by the newspaper's estimate, the amount claimed corresponds to less than 0.1% of the company's shares.

The conflict now has three venues: the Supreme Court's Economic Disputes Chamber on November 10, the new claim in the High Court in London, and the Kaliningrad court through which En+ is enforcing the London award.
