Russia's Finance Ministry is introducing a windfall tax on mining and metals companies. Business does not dispute that it will have to pay. The argument is over what the tax should be based on: rising prices, or profit actually earned.

**What the Finance Ministry proposes.** The bill has been submitted to the State Duma. The rate is 30%, and 20% for gold. The tax is calculated from production volume and from how far the average world price exceeds its 2025 level. The ministry justifies the measure by the sharp rise in world prices for precious and non-ferrous metals and fertilizers.

According to Kommersant, the Finance Ministry expects 683 billion rubles in revenue over 2027–2029. By these calculations, Nornickel would pay the most, 171 billion over three years.

The bill provides for installments. If the tax for a year does not exceed 4% of estimated revenue, the payment within that limit is due by April 28, 2027, and the remainder by April 28, 2029.

**Business's position.** The Russian Union of Industrialists and Entrepreneurs (RSPP) sent the finance minister a letter of objections. In the union's view, the tax is "being introduced retroactively": the base for 2027 is calculated using 2026 prices, and the tax is levied on estimated revenue rather than on profit. Instead, the RSPP proposes temporarily raising corporate income tax by 0.5–1 percentage point, which, by the union's estimate, would bring the budget 0.5 to 1 trillion rubles.

Nornickel president Vladimir Potanin told RBC TV: "You cannot take money in advance; you have to take it from what has been earned, not from what the business does not yet have." He said estimates of the tax's effect are forcing the company to "push some projects to the right." He also objects to the methodology, which in his view does not take into account how revenue is distributed across different products and could hit companies that are losing out because of sanctions even when prices are rising.

At the same time, Potanin notes: "The Finance Ministry is meeting us halfway, offering various kinds of relief."

**The Finance Ministry's position.** Finance Minister Anton Siluanov told reporters on October 5: "We have actually done very good work, met with business and found a compromise on the windfall tax." He added: "We made it so that where there are spikes, we smoothed them out." The minister did not specify which parameters had been changed.

**What doesn't add up.** The minister announced a compromise on October 5. Five days later, Potanin described the question of the tax base this way: "This is now the main subject of discussion with the Finance Ministry." Taken together, the two statements suggest the sides agreed on relief, but not on the principle of calculation.

Business is conducting this argument in public. The RSPP letter became known to the press on October 2, and Potanin's interview came out on October 10.

**What comes next.** The bill has yet to be considered by the State Duma. The parameters Siluanov spoke of should appear in amendments. This page will be updated when their text is known.

**Update, November 16, 2026. First reading passed.** On October 29, the State Duma passed bill No. 618-9 in first reading. The text, as summarized by Interfax, shows how the relief works. The 2025 base price is indexed by factors of 1.1 and 1.21 for 2027 and 2028. No tax is due if the price rose by less than 10% or if there was no extraction at the site in 2025. The total is capped at 15% of revenue for each type of mineral, minus the mineral extraction tax already accrued. Payments within 4% of revenue for 2027 and 5% for 2028 are due by April 28 of the respective year, with the remainder due by April 28, 2029. According to the explanatory note, the tax is expected to bring in about 200 billion rubles a year and take effect on January 1, 2027.

Two days before the vote, on October 27, the Duma's budget and taxes committee backed the bill but proposed that, for the second reading, a company's financial position, profitability and ability to pay be taken into account, rather than defining windfall income by rising prices alone. The committee also proposed discussing the RSPP's idea of excluding technological losses from the base — material sent to waste dumps and tailings storage. That is precisely the question metals producers are arguing about with the Finance Ministry. No date has been set for the second reading.
