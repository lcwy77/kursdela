Friday evening passed to applause. After a phone call between Vladimir Putin and Donald Trump, the U.S. Treasury issued a license for transactions in Russian diesel, and the market answered with fireworks: Rosneft rose more than 9%, LUKOIL 6%, and the Moscow Exchange index approached 2,400 points in the evening session.

The news really is good. Exports mean money, and both the companies and the budget need money. Arguing with that would be foolish.

What bothers me is something else: the tone. The joy is as if we had been given a gift. Yet there is no gift here. Diesel in the U.S. costs a record $6.28 a gallon, the congressional elections are three weeks away, and Europe and the U.S. are tapping their reserves. They needed fuel, and they came for it. This is a deal in which the buyer needs the goods no less than the seller needs the revenue.

And look at the form. We have not been given back a market; we have been issued a temporary license, until April 7, 2027. Another country's ministry has allowed a nation, for six months, to sell its own product, which it sold freely until March 2022. The permission can be extended, or it can be revoked.

We should sell, and we should bargain hard. But to rejoice over someone else's permission is to agree that we need it. A restrained "we have a deal" would suit the country better than delight.
